2020 to the 1950s

Living Memory

A pandemic, a hidden loft rediscovered, a cult film, a TV star behind the bar, and a bar just for children.

2020

Documented

The Doors Close

Like every pub and hotel in the country, the White Swan had to close its doors during the Covid-19 pandemic. It reopened afterwards and carried on as before.

2016

Documented

The Secret Garden

The Secret Garden sign, photographed in June 2016. The garden behind the hotel is the same lawn that was the Swan’s bowling green in Victorian times (see 1855). The Secret Garden is currently closed.

2013

Report

The Hidden Loft, Rediscovered

In September 2013 the Swan’s new owners rediscovered a secret loft space, an old priest hole by repute, and said to be the hideaway Michael Elphick slipped away to in his years behind the bar (see 1980s).

Source: An online profile of Michael Elphick

1990

Hotel history

I Bought a Vampire Motorcycle

The cult British comedy-horror was filmed in Birmingham and reached cinemas in July 1990. Neil Morrissey starred as the bike’s owner, and the Swan’s own landlord, Michael Elphick, played the police inspector.

The Swan’s history says that while the film was being made, the hotel did the catering and put up members of the cast.

Watch on YouTube: I Bought a Vampire Motorcycle: cinema trailer

Source: Film details: published cast and release records. Catering and rooms: the Swan’s own history.

1980s

Documented in part

A TV Star Behind the Bar

The actor Michael Elphick ran the White Swan through the 1980s and into the early 1990s. He is best remembered as Ken Boon in the TV series Boon, which began in 1986. He died in 2002, aged 55.

Rumour has it he kept a hideaway at the Swan: a secret loft space, said to be an old priest hole, where he would go to write letters to his daughter and learn his scripts for Boon (see 2013).

Source: Landlord: the Swan’s own history. The hideaway: an online profile of Michael Elphick

1950s

Hotel history

A Bar Just for Children

Cygnets is the bar attached to the hotel, and it’s still part of the Swan today. For a short time in the 1950s it was set aside as a children’s bar.

Children had been kept out of pub bars by law since the Children Act of 1908. So while the grown-ups had a drink in the main bar, children could sit in Cygnets with a soft drink. The name fits: cygnets are young swans.

1935

Hotel history

The Beams Come Back

In Georgian times it was the fashion to hide timber framing under plaster. When the Swan was renovated in 1935 the plaster came off, and the 16th-century beams were on show again.

1908

Hotel history

A Walk to the Railway

The path beside the hotel through to the railway was made in 1908, the year Henley’s new station opened on the North Warwickshire Line. The station is still only a short walk away.

1903

Documented

The Court Moves Out

Until 1903, the local magistrates held their petty sessions at the White Swan, trying the lesser cases for the Henley district. On 26 August 1903 Henley’s new Courthouse opened, and the court moved there.

Source: Henley-in-Arden Heritage Centre

1855 to the early 1700s

Coaching Days

Bowls on the lawn, a ghost story, a diarist’s bill, Dr Johnson in town, coaches at the gate, and the poem we claim.

1855

Documented in part

Bowls on the Lawn

By 1855, the Swan’s history says, the lawn behind the hotel was a bowling green. The Heritage Centre has a photograph of the original White Swan bowling green in 1891, with the local butcher Harry Hawkes in the picture.

That same lawn is today’s Secret Garden (see 2016).

Source: The Swan’s own history; Henley-in-Arden Heritage Centre

1845

Folklore

The Swan’s Ghost Story

Every old inn has a ghost story, and this is ours. It tells of a young woman named Virginia Black, said to have been eighteen, who died after a fall on the stairs in 1845.

Over the years, guests and staff said they had sensed her in the corridor outside Room 17. By the hotel’s own account she hasn’t been seen in a long time.
1792

Documented

A Diarist’s Bill

John Byng, later Viscount Torrington, kept diaries of his rides around England between 1781 and 1794. The published edition of his diaries records the Swan at Henley-in-Arden.

Jonathan Dovey’s History of Henley-in-Arden says Byng stopped here in July 1792, and that he “stayed at the (White) Swan and had food and drink for 2s 2d”, two shillings and twopence. The Swan’s history adds that he is thought to have first passed through Henley on 5 July 1781.

Source: The Torrington Diaries, ed. C. Bruyn Andrews (1934 to 1938), index; Jonathan Dovey, History of Henley-in-Arden

1776

Documented in part

Dr Johnson Comes to Henley

On 21 March 1776, Dr Samuel Johnson and his biographer James Boswell spent the night in Henley. Boswell wrote that down. He didn’t write down which inn.

They had left Oxford that morning by post-chaise, heading for Birmingham and Johnson’s home town of Lichfield. Over dinner at an inn called Chapel House, Johnson said that “there is nothing which has yet been contrived by man, by which so much happiness is produced as by a good tavern or inn”, and then recited Shenstone’s lines on the warmest welcome at an inn (see 1714–1763). That night they lay at Henley, and the next morning they set out early and reached Birmingham at about nine.Tradition says that they stayed here, and that Johnson sat up working on something while he was here.

Look for itA copy of Johnson’s Dictionary in the Johnson Suite

Source: James Boswell, Life of Johnson (1791); Victoria County History of Warwickshire, vol. 3 (1945)

1726

Documented

Coaches at the Gate

In 1726 a turnpike trust was set up to look after the road from Birmingham to Stratford, which runs through Henley and had been described as “ruinous and bad”. At the height of the coaching age, seven coaches a day passed through the town.

The Swan’s history says it was a well-known 30-mile staging post on the route between Lichfield and London, where coaches stopped to change horses. The wide gateway on the High Street, known as the coach entrance, is still there today.

Look for itThe old coach entrance on the High Street

Source: Henley-in-Arden Heritage Centre; the Swan’s own history

1714–1763

Reported

The Warmest Welcome

“Whoe’er has travell’d life’s dull round, / Where’er his stages may have been, / May sigh to think he still has found / The warmest welcome at an inn.”

The Swan has long claimed that the poet William Shenstone stayed here, and that this is the inn behind his poem “Written at an Inn at Henley”.

Shenstone grew up at the Leasowes in Halesowen, near Birmingham, and studied at Oxford, and the road between the two ran through Henley-in-Arden. His poem praises the freedom of a good inn, and its last verse became one of the most quoted in the language. Dr Johnson knew it by heart (see 1776).

1663 to 1352

The Old Inn

Hearths, a king on the run, barns and stables, and the building you stand in.

1663

Hotel history

Six Hearths, the Most in Town

The Hearth Tax returns of 1663 record six hearths at the White Swan, more than at any other building in Henley at the time.

1651

Reported

A King Disguised as a Servant

The story here is that King Charles II stopped at the Swan in September 1651, on the run after losing the Battle of Worcester and disguised as a servant.

On 10 September he left Bentley Hall in Staffordshire dressed as a tenant farmer’s son and calling himself William Jackson. He rode as the servant of a young woman named Jane Lane, on the same horse as her, and their road ran south through this part of Warwickshire. At Wootton Wawen, two miles down the road from our door, they rode calmly past a troop of Parliament’s cavalry gathered outside the inn, and that night they reached Long Marston, beyond Stratford.

Henley sits on that road, and the Heritage Centre reckons the King most probably stopped here on the way. The Swan has always said he did. We like to think he had our best room, the Diamond Suite.

Look for itThe Diamond Suite, our best room

Source: Accounts of the King’s escape; Henley-in-Arden Heritage Centre, via the Swan’s own history

1608

Documented

“An Inn called the Swan”

A record of August 1608 describes “an Inn called the Swan with barns and stables, orchards and courtyards”.

The Swan’s history names the tenant that year as Tomas Kirby. The Victoria County History links the innkeeper Thomas Wheler, recorded as an innkeeper in 1592, with the Swan in 1608.

Source: The Swan’s own history; Victoria County History of Warwickshire, vol. 3 (1945)

Late 16th century

Documented

The Building You Stand In

The timber-framed building you see today dates from the late 16th century, with its front partly rebuilt in the 1600s. It has been a listed building since 1952, and is now listed at Grade II*.

Look for itThe jettied, timber-framed gable and the stone-mullioned bay windows on the High Street

Source: Victoria County History of Warwickshire, vol. 3 (1945); Historic England

16th century

Reported

Stone from the Castle

When the present building went up, the story goes, its builders took stone from the ruins of Beaudesert Castle across the road and built it into the Swan.

By then the castle had lain in ruins for nearly three hundred years (see 1265), and old stone like that was too useful to leave lying about.

Look for itThe bare stretch of wall by table 9 in the bar, said to be the castle’s wall stone

1265 to before 1066

Before the Inn

A castle across the road, and the crest that may have given the Swan its name.

1265

Documented in part

Fire and Ruin

Peter de Montfort of Beaudesert was killed fighting against Henry III at the Battle of Evesham in 1265. Afterwards Henley was burnt down, perhaps in revenge for the part he had taken against the King.

The Swan’s history adds that the de Montforts’ castle at Beaudesert was destroyed too, and that the town grew up again from the ruins.

Look for itThe Mount, the castle mound by the Church of St Nicholas, across the road

Source: Victoria County History of Warwickshire, vol. 3 (1945); the Swan’s own history

Late 11th century

Hotel history

Beaudesert Castle

Thurstan, the first of the de Montforts of Beaudesert, built a castle on the Mount, across the road from today’s hotel. Henley grew up under its protection.

After 1066

Hotel history

“Beautiful waste”

The Norman settlers named the place Beaudesert. Henley was a small part of the lands held in Wootton Wawen by Robert de Toeni, later known as Robert de Stafford. Heritage Centre records suggest the White Swan probably takes its name from the Staffords’ crest.

Before 1066

Hotel history

Wagen’s Land

Just before the Norman Conquest, the land was held by the Saxon thane Wagen, a name later worn down to Wawen.
